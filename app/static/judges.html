Airbus Challenge #2 · Air traffic decisions

Three teams. One checked arrival plan. A human decision.

How we answer the problem statement

The challenge describes phone calls and manual updates across ATC, pilots and ground staff, with incompatible decisions causing more delay and holding. PLAB.ai gives the duty manager a shared, versioned decision record. The time saved over that manual process has not yet been measured.

Three independent agents

ATC: runway availability, wake spacing, taxi blockages and movement capacity.
Pilot: fuel, permitted arrival conditions and passenger connections; flight-duty limits are also checked.
Ground: gates, handling crews, equipment, overtime and turnaround.

Each role gets a separate model invocation and returns structured JSON. The agents explain preferences and concessions; deterministic code computes schedules, costs and feasibility.

Consensus within three turns

One turn means one message per role. Turn 1 collects offers and concessions; Turn 2 votes on one complete plan; Turn 3 can revise it after dissent. Consensus needs three fresh accepts of the same plan hash. At most nine generation requests, with no hidden retries.

A 45-second run budget bounds waiting. Missing or invalid replies can end negotiation early.

Real flight + explicit operational inputs

The supplied CSV was verified: 358,774 flights from 2015, each with WEATHER_DELAY > 15 or LATE_AIRCRAFT_DELAY > 20. D01 uses AA1057, DFW–MIA, delayed 102 minutes by weather.

DOT supplies the historical trigger. The organiser's synthetic workbook supplies airport inventory, movements/hour, turnaround, wake, fuel and crew rules. Additional traffic, occupancy, fuel state and connections are declared simulation inputs. Source hashes and workbook cells are retained.

A feasible revised decision

Code enumerates up to 124 landing/gate/taxi combinations and applies 15 checks. A plan must pass every required check before it can be reviewed. The output includes landing and gate-in times, gate, route, fuel margin and ground-crew overtime.

Deterministic fallback

If negotiation fails, fixed rules select the earliest feasible option from the checked catalog, using zero model calls. Failed or unknown checks cannot be waived. If no plan is feasible or essential data is missing, the app gives a non-executable outcome with a reason.

Human control + trace

Agreement alone changes nothing. A human supplies a decision reason; fallback additionally needs an explicit acknowledgement. Approval rechecks the exact plan, source versions, current resources and expiry before atomically booking runway, gate, crew and equipment in the simulator.

Messages, checks, counts and decisions are persisted in a downloadable, hash-chained audit log.

Evidence for every judging criterion

These are the supplied rubric weights, not self-awarded scores. Technical soundness, honest evaluation and trust together account for 65%.

Problem realism 20%
A named duty-manager workflow, a qualifying historical disruption and conflicting ATC/Pilot/Ground responsibilities. The prototype covers one arrival at a time. See the problem-to-feature mapping.
Technical soundness 25%
A working path from inputs to a persisted, approved simulation update. Independent LLM messages express preferences; Python owns arithmetic, constraints, selection and application. Try a live run without API keys.
Honest evaluation 20%
A frozen suite, B0/B1 baselines, actual denominators and retained failures. Initial live consensus was 1/6; passing the system's contract checks does not mean the agents succeeded. Read the measured results.
Trust and traceability 20%
Checkable source cells, rule margins, same-plan votes, visible uncertainty and a mandatory human decision. Stale or unsupported plans cannot be applied. Inspect the evidence and audit trail.
Impact 10%
The UI exposes holding, fuel and overtime trade-offs against fair deterministic baselines. Real coordination time and operational savings remain unmeasured; the initial live suite did not improve on B0. See the exact comparison.
Time discipline 5%
A focused presentation route fits within three minutes. Submission timing must come from the hackathon portal; this site does not establish it. Follow the timed walkthrough.

Why the five live cases did not reach agreement

A missing or invalid reply is different from an agent rejecting an operational plan. These explanations come from the recorded role messages and request events.

H01 · ATC timeout
ATC did not reply before the request deadline. Ground submitted an offer; Pilot's pending request was cancelled when the turn stopped. No common proposal reached a vote. Inspect the recorded timeout.
H02 · ATC timeout
Pilot and Ground submitted offers, but ATC again timed out. Two offers cannot substitute for three valid role messages. There was no recorded operational rejection. Inspect each agent's response.
H03 · Pilot needs information
ATC and Ground accepted the negotiated plan. Pilot asked for CREW-G1 availability confirmation instead of accepting. All 15 deterministic checks on that proposal passed; the Pilot's stated concern is not proof of a crew shortage. An information request cannot count as consent, so the coordinator stopped at Turn 2. Read the Pilot's exact stated reason and the 2-of-3 result.
H04 · Ground evidence rejected
Ground cited evidence IDs outside its supplied references. Its reply failed validation before voting. Pilot had offered options; ATC's pending request was cancelled. This was an unverifiable response, not a demonstrated gate conflict. Inspect the validation failure.
H05 · ATC offer rejected
ATC put an option outside its permitted initial preference set into its acceptable choices. The offer failed policy validation; Pilot and Ground requests were cancelled. A plan passing the hard checks does not make it an allowed initial preference. Inspect why the offer was rejected.

Why stop before Turn 3? Three turns is a ceiling. Timeouts, invalid messages and requests for missing information stop the current run early. A valid rejection can trigger Turn 3 only when a different common option and enough time remain. No cancelled or missing reply becomes an acceptance.

Current-version limit: a later roles-v7 development run reached 0/4 consensus, with three prompt-adapter errors and one policy violation. The adapter was then fixed; a focused live smoke still timed out and used fallback. No full live re-evaluation followed. The 1/6 result is historical evidence, not a benchmark of the final prompt.

Impact: a measured trade-off, not a savings claim

B0 selects the earliest feasible plan. B1 minimises the highest role cost, then total cost. Both use the same inputs, rules and full candidate catalog as the agent system.

All six nominal cases in the initial live report matched B0's modeled impacts. No live benefit over that baseline was demonstrated. The scripted D01 third-turn example illustrates a different compromise: 2 extra minutes of holding for 35 fewer minutes of ground-crew overtime. This is a synthetic trade-off, not a measured historical saving or proof that agents beat B1.

Scope limits and the next validation step
  • One runway, two candidate spare gates and a fixed background schedule. This is not airport-wide rescheduling.
  • Movement counts proxy throughput; supplied route-permission labels proxy weather/turbulence. Departure wake times are explicitly reused as an arrival-simulation proxy. Distance separation and full flight-plan changes are outside this MVP.
  • Synthetic operational state cannot establish real-world safety or savings. The small holdout shares scenario templates; it is not a generalization benchmark. The audit chain is inspectable, not tamper-proof against the database owner.
  • Next: verify the corrected prompt and provider latency on a new frozen live evaluation, then validate operational assumptions and review time with practitioners. These steps are not claimed complete.

A three-minute judge walkthrough

The saved examples below are read-only and use scripted messages. Use the interactive demo for live calls and a real human approval within the simulator.

  1. 0:00–0:25 · The user and trigger. Choose D01 and start a run in a separate demo tab. Distinguish DOT history, synthetic workbook limits and simulated current conditions.
  2. 0:25–1:10 · Coordination. Open the third-turn example. Expand each agent's messages: different preferences, Turn 2 dissent, then fresh agreement on the revised plan.
  3. 1:10–1:50 · Human control. Show the checks and decision reason in the live demo. Approve only after review; a fallback also needs acknowledgement. Show the recorded simulation update.
  4. 1:50–2:20 · A visible limit. Open missing fuel data: there is no approvable plan. Explain how timeouts produce a clearly labelled fallback.
  5. 2:20–3:00 · Evidence. Present 1/6 live consensus separately from 12/12 contract cases, explain B0/B1 and the synthetic trade-off, then state the next validation step.
